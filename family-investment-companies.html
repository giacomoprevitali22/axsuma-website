Family Investment Companies

A Practical Route to Multi-Generational Wealth Governance

For decades, the trust was the default answer whenever a UK family wanted to pass down wealth without losing control of it. That default is shifting. As trust taxation has tightened and reporting obligations have multiplied, more families and their advisers are turning to a simpler, more familiar vehicle: the private limited company, repurposed as a Family Investment Company, or FIC.

A FIC is not a special legal category. It is an ordinary company – usually limited by shares, sometimes unlimited for privacy reasons – set up for one purpose: to hold, grow and eventually pass on a family’s investable wealth. What makes it powerful is not the structure itself but how deliberately it can be shaped around a family’s own rules.

Why families are choosing FICs over trusts

A trust separates legal and beneficial ownership through the relationship between settlor, trustee and beneficiary. A FIC does something more intuitive to most business owners: it separates ownership (who holds the shares) from control (who sits on the board and directs the money). Parents or grandparents can gift shares – and therefore future value – to children or grandchildren, while remaining directors and continuing to make every investment decision themselves.

That single feature explains most of the appeal:

  • Control without full ownership. The founding generation can retain voting rights or board authority long after they’ve transferred economic value to the next generation.
  • Tailored share structures. Different classes of shares can carry different rights to income, capital growth or votes – so one child might receive dividend income while another accumulates capital, all from the same pool of assets.
  • A familiar governance model. Company law, board minutes, shareholder agreements and statutory registers are well understood by accountants, banks and HMRC alike, which tends to make FICs easier to explain and administer than a discretionary trust.
  • A degree of asset protection. Restrictions on share transfers outside the family, combined with board-level discipline, can offer some insulation in the event of divorce or family disputes, though this depends heavily on how the articles are drafted.
  • Corporate tax treatment. Investment income and gains sit inside the corporation tax regime rather than personal income tax, and most dividends received by a UK company from other companies are exempt from corporation tax – allowing returns to compound inside the structure before any decision is made to extract them.
  • Inheritance planning built in. Gifting shares to the next generation can start the clock on the usual seven-year period for those shares to fall outside the donor’s estate, while the donor keeps running the company day to day.

None of this makes a FIC a universal solution. Extracting profits still triggers both corporation tax and personal tax on dividends, so FICs suit families who can afford to let value accumulate rather than needing regular income from the assets. Moving existing investments or property into the company can trigger tax charges of its own, and reliefs that apply to individuals – Business Property Relief or the Enterprise Investment Scheme, for example – generally aren’t available once assets sit inside a corporate wrapper. A FIC is also not a “set and forget” structure: it needs proper company secretarial administration, statutory filings, accurate accounts and a board that actually behaves like one.

Where a FIC tends to make the most sense

In practice, FICs come up most often for:

  • Entrepreneurs after a business sale, looking to ring-fence proceeds for the next generation while keeping a firm hand on how it’s invested
  • Families consolidating a scattered portfolio of shares, funds and cash under one governed structure with a single set of accounts
  • Households thinking about inheritance tax exposure who want a lifetime, rather than posthumous, way to pass on value
  • Families who want the discipline of board minutes and reserved matters rather than the comparative informality of managing assets personally
  • Situations where a trust already exists and a FIC is used alongside it, rather than instead of it, to hold non-trading investment assets

Why the administration is the part that actually matters

The legal mechanics of setting up a company are simple. What separates a FIC that works smoothly for thirty years from one that becomes a source of family friction is the quality of the ongoing governance and administration behind it – the parts that rarely make it into the initial conversation with a solicitor, but that determine whether the structure still does its job when the founding generation steps back.

That is where a corporate services provider earns its place at the table. A well-run FIC needs:

  • Formation done properly the first time – bespoke articles of association that actually reflect the family’s intentions on control, voting and succession, rather than a generic template that has to be unpicked later
  • Company secretarial discipline – statutory registers kept current, confirmation statements and accounts filed on time, board minutes that stand up to scrutiny if HMRC or a future adviser ever asks to see them
  • Governance support that flexes with the family – reserved matters, shareholder agreements and decision-making protocols that evolve as children come of age, marry, or join the board themselves
  • Compliance that keeps pace with the rules – including the identity verification and persons-with-significant-control obligations now built into UK company law under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act, which apply to every director and PSC of a FIC just as they do to any other UK company
  • A steady hand for the awkward moments – a director resigning, a loan to the company needing to be documented correctly, a new family member being brought onto the share register

How can Axsuma help?

As a UK-registered Authorised Corporate Service Provider under ECCTA, Axsuma is built around the compliance backbone that every UK company – including a FIC – now has to satisfy: identity verification, PSC registers, and filings that meet Companies House’s tightened standards. Alongside that regulatory grounding, Axsuma supports families and their advisers with company formation and bespoke constitutional documents, company secretarial administration, director and registered office services, and the ongoing filing and governance work that keeps a FIC compliant and presentable year after year – freeing the family, and the lawyers and accountants advising them, to focus on the investment decisions rather than the paperwork behind them.

The advice still comes first

None of this replaces proper legal and tax advice. Whether a FIC is the right answer – and how it should be structured relative to any existing trusts, wills or family business interests – is a question for a family’s solicitor and tax adviser, informed by their specific assets, objectives and appetite for complexity. Once that decision is made, though, the structure only continues to work if someone keeps the engine room running properly. That ongoing administration, compliance and governance support is where Axsuma’s services sit, working alongside the family’s existing advisers rather than in place of them.
